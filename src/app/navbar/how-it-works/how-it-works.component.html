How the Bite Forecast works

When you search a location, we fetch the current conditions and the past five days of weather for it, then score how likely each of your chosen species is to be feeding. The result is a 0–100 activity score, banded from Switched off to Prime, with the biggest factors shown alongside it. Here's exactly what goes into that number.

The starting point: temperature and season

Fish are cold-blooded, so water temperature sets their appetite — it's the strongest-evidenced factor in fish biology. Water warms and cools more slowly than air, so we estimate it from a weighted average of the last few days' air temperatures rather than today's reading alone. Each species has its own comfort bands: carp feed hardest at 12–22 °C and largely stop below 4 °C, chub keep feeding down to near freezing, wels catfish barely feed below 10 °C, and trout go quiet in heat. On top of that, every species has a seasonal rhythm — pike peak from autumn to late winter, catfish in high summer — and the forecast follows it.

The direction of change

Anglers have always known a sharp cold snap kills sport, and the science agrees — it's the single most reliable negative event we score. But change isn't always bad: a warming trend in the cooler months is a classic feeding trigger, and in a summer heatwave a cooling breakdown actually helps. The forecast scores the direction of the temperature trend, not just whether the weather has been steady.

Rain, and why it isn't simply bad

Rain oxygenates the water, washes food in, and adds colour that makes fish feed confidently. For river species the classic prime window is a river fining down — dropping and clearing a day or two after rain. Salmon anglers live by "fresh water brings fresh fish", and a warm, coloured river is the best barbel fishing there is. So recent rain followed by a drier day scores a bonus for those species, while a bone-dry settled week counts against salmon. The genuinely bad rain is cold rain — a downpour arriving with falling temperatures — and the forecast penalises exactly that.

Light, wind and pressure

Cloud cover matters more than most anglers realise — it's one of the few factors with solid catch-rate studies behind it. Overcast skies effectively extend dawn and dusk all day, which suits nearly every species and especially light-shy hunters like zander. The exception is winter, when sunshine warming shallow water is a small positive. Wind is scored two ways: a moderate ripple beats a flat calm, and the compass matters — the old rhyme "wind from the west, fish bite best; wind from the east, fish bite least" encodes the real difference between mild Atlantic air and cold continental air. Barometric pressure gets a deliberately modest weight: controlled studies show fish don't respond to pressure directly (a fish swimming up a foot changes its pressure more than any weather front), but a falling barometer predicts the cloudy, mild, unsettled weather that genuinely does bring fish on the feed.

The welfare warning

Some species are harmed by being caught in hot water: above roughly 19–20 °C trout and salmon struggle to recover after release, and many UK fisheries and river authorities formally ask anglers to stop. Pike suffer similarly in warm water. When our water-temperature estimate crosses those documented thresholds, the forecast doesn't just say "poor" — it says don't fish for them today.

Honest caveats

  • Air temperature is a proxy — we can't measure your actual water, and deep or spring-fed venues will drift from our estimate.
  • We don't know your venue: depth, colour, stock levels and angling pressure all matter and aren't in any weather feed.
  • River species' scores assume the river responds normally to rain; we don't have live river-level data.
  • The score is an outlook, not a promise — time of day, watercraft and bait choice will always beat the weather. Fish dawn and dusk when in doubt.
